# Milo and the Missing Forest Star

Approved narrative and future activity design. The forest environment now uses selected dialogue from `src/data/forestAdventure.ts` around a navigable adventure. Frog, Bear, Baby Snake, Squirrel and Owl now have playable encounters in `src/forest/AnimalActivity.tsx`. This document remains the narrative and activity design reference. The current environment places the Story Tree deep in the forest, following the subsequent UI brief.

## The story in one breath

The little light that guides everyone to the Story Tree has disappeared. Milo follows its golden trail along a winding forest path. Helping five animals gives him a clearer picture of what happened: the Star fell, rolled, and then flew. A young bird carried it home because the nest felt dark. Milo finds a way to light the nest **and** bring the Forest Star back to everyone.

The question changes as the child discovers more:

**Where did it go? → How did it move? → Who carried it? → What did they need? → How can we help both?**

## Geography and continuity

Use the reference image only as a narrative cue: one sandy path curving between trees, gentle rises that hide the next encounter, and quieter clearings along the way. Do not import the image or redesign the current scene in this step.

The Story Tree stands beside the forest entrance. Milo checks its low, seed-shaped Star cradle before taking the path into the woods. The trail makes a gentle loop: puddles → tall trees → winding roots → big oak → old hollow tree → sheltered nest → back to the Story Tree. The **big oak**, **old hollow tree**, and **Story Tree** are distinct landmarks.

The Forest Star is warm-looking, cool to touch, and small enough for a young bird to carry. It leaves faint golden dust when it brushes something. The dust is a physical clue, not collectible treasure.

What actually happened, held back until the ending: a gust rocked the cradle and the Star fell. It bounced beside Frog's puddles, brushed a low branch near Bear, and rolled down the winding trail past Baby Snake. Near the oak, a young bird found what looked like a lost glowing seed and carried it toward the hollow tree. Squirrel heard the takeoff; Owl heard the wings pass overhead. No animal is hiding information, and no villain is revealed.

At the entrance, establish that Milo brought a small, cool-glowing lantern for the walk home. This makes the final solution something the child has already seen, rather than an object appearing conveniently at the last moment. There is no fire or fuel interaction.

## Opening — the empty cradle

Late afternoon. The Story Tree is familiar, but its little cradle is empty. Milo has his lantern for the journey home.

**Milo:** “That’s strange…”

**Milo:** “The Forest Star was right here.”

A golden scuff on the cradle leads to a dotted, curving trail in the earth. Milo notices it; he does not explain the whole mystery.

**Milo:** “Maybe someone in the forest saw where it went.”

The trail disappears around a bend. We hear a small **boing** before we meet its owner.

## 1. Frog — across the puddles

**Personality:** Playful, welcoming, delighted by different-sized hops.

**Small problem:** Frog knows the stones, but Milo does not know how to cross the muddy patch. Frog offers to show him.

**Frog:** “Boing! Boing!”

**Milo:** “How are you getting across?”

**Frog:** “Like this! Can you jump like me?”

Frog demonstrates once, then waits. The child tries two jumps, then three, a big imaginary frog jump, and a tiny one. Each is its own short invitation. The screen does not keep performing while the child watches. Finger hops or seated bounces are equally welcome; there is no movement recognition or performance judgement.

As Milo crosses, the last stone turns slightly. A little gold dust catches the light.

**Frog:** “You’re pretty good at being a frog.”

**Frog:** “Oh! I saw something glowing go past here!”

**Frog:** “It went toward the tall trees.”

**Milo:** “A little glow, just like our Star. Let’s look.”

**Mystery advances:** The Star came this way. The tall trees give Milo a specific destination.

## 2. Bear — a little higher

**Personality:** Gentle, patient, speaks in short, thoughtful bursts.

**Small problem:** Bear hung a little honey pot in a tree for a picnic and cannot reach it from the ground. Avoid disturbing a hive or bees.

**Bear:** “I can smell it…”

**Milo:** “Your honey?”

**Bear:** “Too high.”

Bear stretches. The child reaches upward with feet on the floor, then stretches a little taller if comfortable. This lets them feel the problem; it does not magically lower the honey.

Milo notices three sturdy branches at reachable intervals. The child chooses the lowest branch first, then the middle, then the highest. **Bear** does the climbing; the child only points or taps.

As Bear comes down with the pot, a bent twig springs back and shows a golden smear.

**Bear:** “You helped me reach it!”

**Bear:** “Wait… I saw something shiny near the winding trail.”

**Milo:** “Another golden mark. It went around that bend.”

**Mystery advances:** The marks connect the puddles to the winding trail. Seeing a mark at ground level still fits a bouncing or rolling object.

## 3. Baby Snake — zig, then zag

**Personality:** Earnest, a little muddled, proud when a pattern begins to make sense.

**Small problem:** Baby Snake is trying to follow the curved path to its family. A parent is visible just beyond the roots, calmly waiting. This is a playful navigation problem, not a lost-child scare.

**Baby Snake:** “They went zig…”

**Baby Snake:** “…or was it zag?”

**Milo:** “Let’s follow the bends together.”

The child sways left, right, left, right; predicts right after left–right–left; then invents a little snake wiggle. A front-facing demonstration uses the child’s own left/right consistently. A hand wiggle is enough.

The successful turn brings Snake beside a faint rolling groove. Its family waits at the next bend.

**Baby Snake:** “I remember something! A glowing thing rolled past me.”

**Baby Snake:** “It went toward the big oak tree.”

**Milo:** “So it rolled! Let’s follow that little line.”

**Mystery advances:** We now know how the Star moved. The groove leads directly to Squirrel's oak.

## 4. Squirrel — the places we remember

**Personality:** Busy and forgetful, but never frantic enough to make the scene stressful.

**Small problem:** Squirrel remembers hiding acorns around the oak, but not exactly where.

**Squirrel:** “Acorn… acorn…”

**Milo:** “Did you lose something too?”

**Squirrel:** “I hid my acorns.”

Pause.

**Squirrel:** “I forgot where.”

A breeze lifts the loose leaves, revealing two acorns, or three in the harder version. The child gets a calm look. When ready, they let the leaves settle, then help Squirrel search at the rock, stump, flowers, and bush. Every hidden acorn must be found; the first correct place does not end the encounter. Repeating the same place never counts twice. A leaf edge or acorn tip offers a clue if needed.

At the last hiding place, Milo notices the rolling groove stops beside the oak.

**Squirrel:** “That’s where they were!”

**Squirrel:** “Oh! When I was hiding them, I heard WHOOSH!”

**Squirrel:** “Something flew toward the old tree.”

**Milo:** “It rolled here… then something flew away?”

**Mystery advances:** The child has a reason to revise the rolling-only explanation. We do not reveal the bird yet.

## 5. Owl — a sound in the quiet

**Personality:** Unhurried and observant; comfortable saying what is known and what is not.

**Small problem:** Owl heard a visitor but could not see it behind the leaves. Milo and Owl compare sounds to work out what passed.

The light is cooler, but the path remains readable and safe-looking.

**Owl:** “Who goes there?”

**Milo:** “We’re looking for the Forest Star.”

**Owl:** “I didn’t see it. But I heard something.”

Hear leaves, wings, and water separately, with a pause between them. Then replay the sound Owl remembers: a short flutter of wings. The child matches that sound, then considers a rabbit, a bird, and a fish.

**Milo:** “Who could have made that sound?”

The intended answer is a bird. Owl's next line stays specific to the evidence rather than claiming to know everything.

**Owl:** “Yes, wings. And look—a feather.”

**Owl:** “Follow the feathers.”

**Milo:** “Maybe someone carried our Star.”

**Mystery advances:** The sound and feather support a carrier. They lead to the nest; they do not prove bad intentions.

For sound-off play or hearing access, offer a clearly described *looking version*: compare moving leaves, fluttering wings, and rippling water, then use the feather. It advances the same story without pretending to assess listening.

## Final discovery — two places need a light

A young bird is curled beside the Star in a sheltered nest just beyond the hollow tree. The bird can make short flights but is still small and uneasy in the dark. Its family is nearby.

**Milo:** “There it is!”

He notices the bird and softens.

**Milo:** “Oh… you wanted a little light.”

**Bird:** “It looked like a lost seed. My nest was dark.”

**Bird:** “I didn’t know it lit the forest path.”

Show a short, gentle recollection: wind nudges the Star from its cradle; it bounces, rolls, and stops at the oak; the bird picks it up and flies home. This resolves the changing clues without asking the child to read an explanation.

**Milo:** “The forest needs its Star. What could light your nest?”

Offer the Forest Star, Milo’s lantern, and an apple. Let the child consider both needs. If they pick the Star, acknowledge that it does make light, then show that the forest path would still be dark. If they pick the apple, acknowledge its use as food and return attention to light. Neither choice receives a buzzer or rejection label.

The lantern provides a second light. Milo offers it; Bird places the Star back in Milo's hands willingly.

**Bird:** “A little light for me?”

**Milo:** “And the Forest Star for everyone.”

**Mystery resolves:** Finding the object was only part of helping. Understanding the bird makes the solution possible.

## Ending — the way back glows

Milo takes the short return bend to the Story Tree and places the Star in its cradle. Sunset settles. Warm pools of light gently reveal the familiar path; the nest has its own small light.

The animals acknowledge Milo as the path passes their homes: one Frog hop, Bear's wave, Snake's wiggle, Squirrel's acorn, Owl watching. Stage these one at a time, not as a crowd of simultaneous animations.

**Milo:** “We found it.”

**Milo:** “We couldn’t have done it without everyone.”

Hold on the Forest Star above the Story Tree. Let this feel finished. A quiet walk home is available; there is no autoplay into a second adventure.

## Rules for the later implementation

- Keep the exact existing Milo component and identity. The story does not require a redesign.
- Use one short spoken line and matching text at a time. Sound buttons replay; no microphone or spoken-command response is implied.
- Movement invitations wait for the child. Each has an explicit “Done” continuation and a seated/hand alternative. Never require jumping higher or farther to succeed.
- Help is not a price paid for information. Clues become noticeable through the shared situation. If a child skips a movement or needs assistance, Milo and the animal demonstrate together and the same clue remains available.
- Let memory observation be child-paced. No countdown, speed bonus, or forced four-second hide.
- Track repeated choices distinctly. Complete memory only after all required locations have been found; complete a sequence only after all of its steps.
- Preserve partial progress during a gentle retry. Offer increasingly visible environmental clues, then “Show me” help.
- Use modest state notes: which encounter we reached, what changed in the world, and which clues were witnessed. No new persistence or analytics system is needed for this design step.
- Aim for roughly 15–25 minutes with free exploration and movement, but treat that as a playtest hypothesis. Never pad the story or enforce a duration.
